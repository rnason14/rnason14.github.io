The Entre­preneurial Mindset The Entre­preneurial Mindset

A McGill course about noticing problems and doing something about them with whatever you have on hand. Students practise it through exercises, site visits and conversations with people who built things in very different corners of Montréal.

You don't need a startup idea, and you don't need to be in Management. Most people who take this course won't found a company. They'll bring the habit into a lab, a clinic, a studio or an organization someone else started.

Entrepreneurs come in more kinds than the headlines suggest

The usual picture is a tech founder with a pitch deck. That's one version. This course looks at many of them, and at the habits of mind they have in common.

  • The artist

    A musician, designer or performer making a living from their craft and building an audience around it.

  • The scientist

    A researcher taking an invention out of the lab and finding the people who need it.

  • The organizer

    An activist or nonprofit founder pulling people and resources together to change something in their community.

  • The professional

    A dentist, lawyer or physiotherapist opening a practice of their own.

  • The intrapreneur

    An employee who spots an opening inside a large organization and starts something new there.

  • The founder

    Yes, also the person starting a venture from nothing. They're welcome here too.

How the course runs

The design draws on Bandura's work on self-efficacy: confidence grows through doing, watching others do, encouragement and reflection. Each class mixes all four.

Democratize

Entrepreneurial thinking belongs to every discipline. The aim is to find where it fits your path, whatever that path is.

Play. Do. Learn.

Most classes include a hands-on exercise that puts the idea to use the same afternoon, in a setting where getting it wrong costs little.

Interdisciplinary

The room is deliberately mixed. English, chemistry, engineering, music and management students work on the same problems.

Human-centered

Start from what people need. Take the humanity of entrepreneurs seriously too, including the stress and the setbacks.

Field Notes by hand

Every student gets a pocket sketchpad for raw observations: pain points, odd questions, half-formed ideas. It stays private and analog.

Reflection journal

Entries start from something that frustrated you or seemed broken, worked through with three prompts: What? So what? Now what?

Entrepreneur interview

Pick someone who built something, in any field, and talk to them about the turning points. Then read their story through the ideas from class.

AI, used openly

Students can use AI tools on assignments with a short note on how. Do some thinking first, check what it gives you, and own the result.

Each term ends with something the class runs for the whole campus

There is no fixed format. It could be a design jam, a problem challenge, a pop-up store or something nobody has tried yet. The students decide, and they plan it in public.

  1. Everyone sketches ideas, forms teams around the strongest ones and pitches them to the room.
  2. The top concepts leave the classroom and get tested on people outside the course.
  3. The class votes, and the winner gets built over the rest of the term.

Questions people ask

Something missing? Email robert.nason@mcgill.ca.

Do I need a business idea to take this?

No. The course is about how you notice and act on problems, wherever you end up. Plenty of students arrive without any plan to start something, and that's expected.

Who can register?

The course is built for students outside the Faculty of Management. Management students who are keen should email Professor Nason directly.

When is it offered?

In the fall term. Version 1 is running in Fall 2026, and future offerings will be posted here once they're confirmed.

Is there a textbook or a final exam?

Neither. Readings are short and mostly free: articles, longform journalism, the occasional chapter excerpt. Grades come from participation, reflection, an interview, the campus event and a final synthesis paper.

How much of the course is lecture?

Not much. A typical class has a short framing of the idea, an exercise, a guest or a site visit, and time to compare notes afterward. Guests are meant to be thought-provoking rather than gospel, and they often disagree with each other.

Who supports the course?

BUSA 303 is offered by the Desautels Faculty of Management with support from the John Dobson Foundation, and connects students to the wider McGill and Montréal innovation community.
